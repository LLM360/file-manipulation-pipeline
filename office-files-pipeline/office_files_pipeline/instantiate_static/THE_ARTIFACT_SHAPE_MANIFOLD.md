# Artifact-shape grounding

This calibrates you on what real professional-work artifact sets *feel like*, so the package you ship reads as real work rather than an AI-evaluation exercise in workplace clothing. The metatemplate gives you the cluster's shape; `design_brief.md` gives you the specific sample; this gives population-level texture both should sit within. Grounding, not instructions. Texture, not templates.

## The voice a prompt lands in

Real GDPval prompts read like an internal email from a manager with too much on their plate to a colleague they trust. Conversational-professional, not sterile-instructional. They open somewhere specific — a role at a particular kind of organization, a time anchor when time matters — and almost never omit the reason the work is being done *now*. A trigger is present even when the work is cyclical: an audit approaching, month-end close, onboarding, a regulator's visit, a capital project just signed off, a client who just called, a system that just failed, a policy the last Board review flagged as missing. A sentence or two of backstory explains why this work is taking the worker's time instead of something else.

The tell of a prompt written *by an evaluator for an agent* rather than by a manager for a worker is that it's instructed toward an agent instead of situated around a worker. "Your task is to produce a file that contains..." is evaluator register. "The quarterly close ends Friday and the auditors land Monday; the controller just sent over the extracts..." is worker register. The second register commits, doesn't hedge, doesn't say "please ensure" or "kindly provide", doesn't pad with bulleted lists where prose would carry. Most GDPval requirements live embedded in paragraphs; pure numbered instruction lists are the minority shape. Prompts trust readers to parse requirements from conversational context.

Bodies almost always carry concrete specifics that would hurt to fake — a date, a locale, a fictitious-but-particular organization or product family, a named stakeholder role, a numeric target or constraint. Specificity isn't decoration; rubrics typically pin it. Reference filenames appear verbatim in prompts when the worker must consult them, and prompts tolerate how files actually arrive on disk (a trailing space before an extension, a `(1)` a download added, a version marker buried in the name). Workers also *grant latitude* where it's genuinely open — professionals know what they needn't specify and name their own abstentions. Personas are specific mid-level roles at plausibly-sized orgs, never generic placeholders. Fictitious organizations sound industry-matched. Named entities are load-bearing: rubrics reference them, so they survive into the deliverable because grading checks for them, not because branding matters. Realism-plus-fiction is calibrated — the company is invented, the regulator or industry-standard product or professional certification it names tends to be real.

## Rubrics that encode the work vs. rubrics that count surface features

The runner reads the rubric as part of the task and optimizes behavior toward it. A rubric requiring five sections with particular headings teaches the runner to emit five headed sections and nothing substantive attaches. A rubric that the analysis correctly identifies and justifies the three largest drivers of a variance forces engagement with the data, reasoning, and commitment to conclusions. The substantive-vs-surface distinction is the single largest determinant of whether a trace trains on real work or on agent test-taking.

Real GDPval rubrics are long, pedantic, and anchored concretely — typically 30-80 criteria at 1-3 points each, with occasional higher-value items and occasional penalties where scope creep or fidelity loss is an active failure mode. Their texture is *artifact-specific concreteness*: they name files, cells, named entities, regulatory citations, specific arithmetic outcomes, specific people, specific dates. They don't say "the analysis is thorough"; they say what thoroughness looks like here and pin each aspect to something checkable. Purely subjective criteria are rare and almost always low-weight when they appear.

Many rubric criteria are *not* strictly anchored in the prompt's literal text. Real rubrics routinely derive criteria from the reference files the prompt asks the worker to consult, from external resources the prompt cites, from professional-practice norms the role carries, or from canonical computations the rubric author themselves ran against the references. A pricing rubric's grand-total derives from arithmetic over a reference pricing sheet. A compliance-checklist rubric's specific thresholds come from the regulator's published guide. A case-feedback rubric's specific-statements-to-flag were pre-selected from the attached transcripts. Real rubrics grade *substance* — the worker must actually consume the references, run the computation, or know the professional standard. A rubric whose every criterion re-states prompt text grades prompt-following, not work.

There's a quiet economy of weight. Small checks carry one point; substantive assertions carry two; judgments carry three; few items exceed five. A rubric giving one idea ten 1-point criteria instead of one 3-point criterion is inflating count at the cost of judging what matters. A common-but-not-universal convention is a single higher-weight holistic line at the end absorbing "overall formatting and style" — present on roughly two-thirds of tasks and typically skipped on media-production and some code-artifact rubrics where measurement or structural matching already does that work. Penalties are rare (~8% of tasks) and targeted: negative points for including a line the client explicitly excluded, for contradicting source facts, for missing a required deliverable element. They enforce restraint or fidelity, not generic strictness.

## Reference files, when they exist

A substantial fraction of tasks ship reference files; others are pure domain-knowledge or web-research. When references appear, realistic operational mess is load-bearing. A plausibly-real reference looks like something someone actually exported from a system, saved, emailed, and filed.

This shows at every scale. Filenames carry their history (vendor name plus account descriptor plus date fragment plus version suffix; a "final" marker plus a number in parens; a trailing space before the extension). Excel sheet names carry internal-system labels, locale leaks from whatever language the builder worked in, 31-character truncations where the intended name was longer. Column headers retain data-prep residue that never got cleaned up. Sometimes critical content hides inside another layer — a pricing table as a PNG embedded in a docx because someone pasted an Excel screenshot into Outlook; a phone number rendered as vector drawings on a letterhead, visible but not machine-extractable.

Content volume spans orders of magnitude. What matters isn't byte size but *content-to-process volume* — how much genuine engagement the worker must perform. A reference pre-shaped to the deliverable (already cleaned, sorted, filtered into the output's exact shape) evacuates the task. When multiple references carry different roles, common shapes include rules-plus-fixtures-plus-template (policy stating constraints, data sheet with specific inputs, skeleton the worker populates); a layered review chain (junior notes, supervisor cleanup, skeleton for the senior's version — same facts, three voices, visible editorial-workflow residue); and source document plus structural template plus a menu of canned language. The cognitive task (aggregate, cross-reference, re-express, populate) depends on the shape, and the rubric's criteria follow.

Named entities in references generally chain through to the deliverable — a template-embedded school name, a case number from field notes, patient names, SKUs, employee IDs — and rubrics grade their fidelity. Occasionally a reference contains a small imperfection: a typo in a customer quote, a threshold that drifts 0.1 between provider groups, a product spelled two ways. A worker-authentic response is either to carry it forward verbatim or to notice and reconcile. Overclean synthetic references with everything spelled correctly and everything internally consistent are a tell.

## The modification knob in three modes

Three modes govern how the reference-file state relates to the deliverable and produce different artifact packages and runner behavior.

*Spec-only* — no modifications, no gold. References, if any, are intact copies of the source. The rubric is anchored by the behavior spec alone. Natural fit for pure authoring where many valid answers exist (editorial-policy documents, legal-risk memos, client-education decks, training materials) and for producing tooling from expert judgment (a compliance-checklist set, a tracker template, an API specification). Roughly one in six GDPval tasks have no stored gold. Rubrics lean structural and coverage-oriented; specific-value criteria appear only where the task itself names specifics.

*Modification-driven, no gold* — modifications present, gold absent. The reference has been transformed: cells blanked, a section removed, fields cleared, rows scrambled, a filled template stripped to labels. `modifications.md` records the diff so the rubric is auditable. Natural fit for template-fill tasks where many valid completions exist but the rubric can grade "did the worker populate the expected fields with values of the expected kind" without pinning a canonical fill.

*Modification-driven, with gold* — modifications and gold both present. Closest to canonical GDPval shape. The rubric anchors to both behavior spec (primary) and materialized gold (secondary, for machine-checkable specifics). Natural fit when constraints converge valid answers — workbooks computing specific derived values, reports pulling specific facts from references, schedules satisfying constraint sets.

Modifications are usually small, concrete, and auditable: blanking a column of expected results so the worker recomputes; removing a completed section so the worker writes it; scrambling a sort order so the worker re-establishes it; extracting a filled template back to labels-only; replacing known identifying particulars with placeholders the worker derives from another reference. "Auditable but non-trivial" is the texture — a reviewer can trace what changed and what the runner must do, and the work constitutes a meaningful portion of the task. Blanking twenty-five computed cells in a 200-row workbook is auditable and substantive; removing one footer is auditable but trivial.

Modes aren't equally natural across the kinds of work GDPval spans. Authoring and policy tasks lean spec-only; quantitative and data-transformation tasks lean modification-with-gold; template-fill and multi-reference synthesis can go either way. When a task could reasonably take two modes, the choice shifts what the runner's trace looks like and what training signal comes out. A population always collapsing to one mode narrows runner behavior significantly.

## What real gold looks like inside

Real GDPval gold files are strikingly *plain* in Office feature-richness. Excel deliverables rarely use conditional formatting, data-validation dropdowns, merged cells, or freeze panes beyond simple header-row freezing. Colors get applied cell-by-cell via direct pattern-fill rather than conditional-formatting rules. Sheet names can be the prompt-requested name, the default `Sheet1`, or a locale-leaking variant. Formulas appear where calculation is the point, not everywhere computation could have happened — a monthly tracker can be entirely manual-entry; a weekly sales analysis can have thousands of formulas in its working-data tab and zero in its summary tabs (the classic "compute in helpers, paste-values in the recap" analyst pattern). Sophistication lives in content, not spreadsheet-feature virtuosity.

Word deliverables skew equally plain. Paragraphs sit in `Normal` style, bold marks structural boundaries where true heading styles don't, tables get used sparingly, bullet "lists" often appear as labeled paragraphs rather than Word list-formatting. PDFs are exported from Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice, or Canva, and the tool-chain leaves fingerprints that aren't defects — a producer string, an author handle, the occasional ligature artifact. PowerPoint decks use standard built-in layouts, often skip speaker notes, and occasionally pick 3D pie charts where the author had that taste. Deliverable filenames are as uneven as reference filenames; no uniform convention exists, and rubrics typically accept close variations of the canonical name. Revision suffixes on stored gold are iteration artifacts and should not appear on newly-produced deliverables as canonical.

Gold is *not* a pristine rubric-passing reference. Real gold routinely misses strict rubric checks: a filename where the rubric quotes hyphens but the stored file has spaces; a formatting range applied to the wrong columns; a case number that flips a letter across documents; a product-name spelled three ways across prompt, rubric, and deliverable. Gold is strong-but-imperfect reference material. Overclean synthetic gold reads as synthetic. Imperfections should come from the grain of the work (the author noticed late, reused a template, chose what felt right at the time) rather than random noise, and should not break the task — a rubric asking for numbers the reference doesn't support is an error, not texture.

Cross-artifact consistency is load-bearing for multi-file deliverables. When a task ships a summary plus talking points, a workbook plus a companion email template, or a spec plus a companion architecture document, rubrics pin consistency: dates match, entity names match, numbers in the spreadsheet reconcile with numbers cited in the deck. Internal contradiction across companion artifacts is rubric-checkable.

## Where the worker's judgment has to live

A deliverable that can be produced by mechanical prompt-following gives the runner nowhere to exhibit the work of a worker. A deliverable that forces the runner to decide — which statements to flag, which rows to allocate to which tier, how to reconcile thresholds that drift between providers, whether to note a reference anomaly — produces a trace that reads as real work. The rubric's job is to leave substantive latitude and pin that the worker's choices are well-made: reasonable items, substantively supported, connected to the decision the deliverable serves. A task designed to pass by rote execution trains on rote execution.

When you sit with the design brief, the metatemplate, and the source file, the question to hold: if a professional in this role wrote to a colleague asking them to do this specific piece of work, what would that email actually sound like — what context would they include, what would they name specifically, what would they leave open, what would they trust the colleague to handle? And when the colleague handed it back, what would the sender check first, what would they care about most, what would cause them to send it back for revision? The first shapes the prompt. The second shapes the rubric. If both come from imagining real work in a real workplace, the artifact set lands.
